Welcome

A house that grew into a map of the world

In Staroye Arakchino, a former village on the western edge of Kazan, the painter Ildar Khanov began building beside his parents’ wooden house in 1994. He had no architect and no fixed plan. He wanted one building where the art of every faith could stand together. Thirty years later his brother Ilgiz is still adding rooms.

From the road it looks like a city skyline squeezed onto one plot: an onion dome next to a minaret, a pagoda roof beside a golden dome. Inside, each hall belongs to a tradition, and each tradition comes from somewhere. Orthodox icons point to Istanbul, the dancing Shiva to Varanasi, the gilded Buddha to Bodh Gaya, the Olmec heads to Mexico.

This atlas unfolds those lines, measures them, and lets you walk through the rooms they come from.

It is a cultural centre and museum, not a place of worship. That is part of its charm: visitors of any faith, or none, walk through the same doors and look at the same walls.

Site and situation

Why here?

The temple stands on a narrow strip of old village land. The Volga, widened since 1955–57 into the Kuybyshev Reservoir, lies in front, and the railway to Moscow runs right beside it. Inside the walls is the wooden house Ildar Khanov was born in.

Its position is perfect for visitors: on the road and railway west of Kazan, 8.2 km from the Kremlin, about 20 km from the island town of Sviyazhsk and 19 km from the Raifa Monastery. Tour buses link all of them into one day on the Volga.

Chorus, silence, neighbours

Three ways to build for many faiths

Silence

A quiet room with every symbol removed, like the UN Meditation Room in New York.

Neighbours

Working houses of worship side by side: Bern (2014), Abu Dhabi (2023), Berlin (planned).

Chorus

The art of every tradition together in one building. This is Kazan.

Among the eleven buildings we compared, Kazan is the only one built by one artist’s family around their own home over three decades. It looks like a “neighbours” building, works like a museum, and was made the way lone visionaries build, like the French postman Ferdinand Cheval and his Ideal Palace.

About this atlas

How it was made

Made in Dhaka. The facts come from a dated fact base of 136 sources, including the scholarly study by V. V. Barashkov (2025), local reporting from Kazan, Russpass, culture.ru and UNESCO. Distances are our own haversine calculations from 55.8006° N, 48.9750° E. Maps use Natural Earth data. Footage and photographs come from the project collection; maps, charts, animations and the film are the project’s own work.
